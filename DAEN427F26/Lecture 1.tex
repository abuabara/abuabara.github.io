\begin{frame}{}
A decision problem begins with a \textbf{choice to be made}\\
under certain \textbf{objectives} and \textbf{constraints}.\\
\begin{block}{Risk and Uncertainty}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Choice under risk}: Outcome probabilities are known\\(or can be estimated reliably).
\item \textbf{Choice under uncertainty}: Outcome probabilities are unknown (or difficult to estimate).
\end{itemize}
\end{block}
\end{frame}

% Risk = known probabilities
% Uncertainty = unknown probabilities

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{}
\begin{block}{Decision and Risk Analysis}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Modeling Decisions}: Define the available choices or actions.
\item \textbf{Modeling Risk}: Analyze situations where outcome probabilities are known.
\item \textbf{Modeling Uncertainty}: Analyze situations where outcome probabilities are unknown (or difficult to estimate).
\item \textbf{Modeling Preferences}: Represent how decision makers value outcomes and trade-offs.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{}
\begin{block}{Decision and Risk Analysis, Examples}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Modeling Decisions}\\A student chooses between studying for an exam tonight or attending a social event.
\item \textbf{Modeling Risk}\\A company launches a new product knowing there is a 70\% chance of high demand and a 30\% chance of low demand.
\item \textbf{Modeling Uncertainty}\\A farmer decides what crop to plant without knowing how much rain will fall this season.
\item \textbf{Modeling Preferences}\\A traveler chooses between a cheaper flight with a long layover and a more expensive direct flight.
\end{enumerate}
\end{block}
\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{}
\begin{block}{Bayesian Modeling for Decision Analysis}
\begin{itemize}
\item Bayesian modeling provides a way to update beliefs as new information becomes available.
\item It combines:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Prior beliefs}: what we know / assume before seeing new data.
\item \textbf{Evidence/data}: new information relevant to the decision.
\item \textbf{Posterior beliefs}: updated probabilities after incorporating the evidence.
\end{enumerate}
\item In decision analysis, Bayesian modeling helps decision makers revise risk estimates and make better choices under uncertainty.
\item In short, it supports learning from evidence and improving decisions over time.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{}
\begin{block}{Bayesian vs.\ Frequentist Thinking}
\footnotesize
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}

\begin{tabular}
{@{}p{1.4cm}
>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.65cm}
>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{4.15cm}@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Frequentist} & \textbf{Bayesian} \\
\midrule
Probability
& Long-run frequency
& Degree of belief \\ \hline
Fixed
& True parameter
& Observed data \\ \hline
Varies
& Sample data
& Belief about parameter \\ \hline
Main idea
& ``What happens in repeated trials?''
& ``What should I believe now?'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{itemize}
\item[] \textbf{Is this coin fair?}
\item \textbf{Frequentist:} ``If the coin were fair, how surprising would these flips be?''
\item \textbf{Bayesian:} ``Given these flips, how much should I believe the coin is fair?''
\end{itemize}
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{}
\begin{block}{Quiz 1}
When flipping a fair coin, we say that "the probability of flipping Heads is 0.5." How do you interpret this probability?
\begin{itemize}
\item[a.] If I flip this coin over and over, roughly 50\% will be Heads.
\item[b.] Heads and Tails are equally plausible.
\item[c.] Both a and b make sense.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{}
\begin{block}{Quiz 2}
An election is coming up and a pollster claims that candidate A has a 0.9 probability of winning. How do you interpret this probability?
\begin{itemize}
\item[a.] If we observe the election over and over, candidate A will win roughly 90\% of the time.
\item[b.] Candidate A is much more likely to win than to lose.
\item[c.] The pollster’s calculation is wrong. Candidate A will either win or lose, thus their probability of winning can only be 0 or 1.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}
\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{}
\begin{block}{Quiz 3}
Consider two claims:
\begin{itemize}
\item[1] Zuofu claims that he can predict the outcome of a coin flip. To test his claim, you flip a fair coin 10 times and he correctly predicts all 10.
\item[2] Kavya claims that she can distinguish natural and artificial sweeteners. To test her claim, you give her 10 sweetener samples and she correctly identifies each.
\end{itemize}

In light of these experiments, what do you conclude?

\begin{itemize}
\item[a.] You’re more confident in Kavya’s claim than Zuofu’s claim.
\item[b.] The evidence supporting Zuofu’s claim is just as strong as the evidence supporting Kavya’s claim.
\end{itemize}
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\item[b.] If in fact I don’t have the disease, what’s the chance that I would’ve gotten this positive test result?
\end{itemize}
\end{block}
\end{frame}
