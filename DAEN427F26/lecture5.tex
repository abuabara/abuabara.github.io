\begin{frame}{Example}

A company has deployed a new ETL pipeline.

\vspace{0.4cm}

Let
\[
\pi =
\Pr(\text{a randomly selected record passes all validation checks}).
\]

\vspace{0.3cm}

You are performing a quality audit.

\vspace{0.3cm}

The goal is to estimate the unknown pass rate $\pi$ as validation
results arrive in batches.

\end{frame}

Three analysts begin with different prior beliefs about $\pi$.

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Bayesian Updating Rule}

Suppose

\[
\pi \sim \mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta),
\]

and

\[
Y \mid \pi \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n,\pi).
\]

After observing $y$ passing records out of $n$ checked records,

\[
\pi \mid y
\sim
\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha+y,\beta+n-y).
\]

\vspace{0.3cm}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\alpha$ represents prior weight associated with passing records.
  \item $\beta$ represents prior weight associated with failing records.
  \item $\alpha+\beta$ represents the effective strength of the prior.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Batch 1: First Audit Results}

Inspect 10 records:

\[
y_1 = 6,
\qquad
n_1 = 10.
\]

Therefore, the observed pass rate is

\[
\frac{6}{10}=0.60.
\]

\vspace{0.4cm}

Each analyst updates their prior according to

\[
\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
\longrightarrow
\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha+6,\beta+4).
\]

\end{frame}

\end{quote}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{}

The posterior mean can be written as

\[
\mathbb{E}(\pi\mid y)
=
\frac{\alpha+\beta}
     {\alpha+\beta+n}
\mathbb{E}(\pi)
+
\frac{n}
     {\alpha+\beta+n}
\frac{y}{n}.
\]

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Why? $^\text{(the Posterior Mean Is a Weighted Mean)}$}
Start with the Beta--Binomial posterior:
\[
\pi \mid y
\sim
\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha+y,\beta+n-y).
\]

Therefore,
\[
\mathbb{E}(\pi \mid y)
=
\frac{\alpha+y}{\alpha+\beta+n}.
\]

Rewrite the numerator as two pieces:
\[
\frac{\alpha+y}{\alpha+\beta+n}
=
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta+n}
+
\frac{y}{\alpha+\beta+n}.
\]

\pause

\vspace{.4cm}
Multiply and divide each piece by a useful quantity:
\[
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta+n}
=
\frac{\textcolor{red}{\alpha+\beta}}{\alpha+\beta+n}
\frac{\alpha}{\textcolor{red}{\alpha+\beta}},
\text{\quad and \quad}
\frac{y}{\alpha+\beta+n}
=
\frac{\textcolor{red}{n}}{\alpha+\beta+n}
\frac{y}{\textcolor{red}{n}}.
\]

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{}
Putting the pieces together,
\[
\mathbb{E}(\pi\mid y)
=
\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha+\beta+n}
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}
+
\frac{n}{\alpha+\beta+n}
\frac{y}{n}.
\]

Since
\[
\mathbb{E}(\pi)=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta},
\]

we get
\[
\boxed{
\mathbb{E}(\pi\mid y)
=
\underbrace{
\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha+\beta+n}
}_{\text{prior weight}}
\underbrace{
\mathbb{E}(\pi)
}_{\text{prior mean}}
+
\underbrace{
\frac{n}{\alpha+\beta+n}
}_{\text{data weight}}
\underbrace{
\frac{y}{n}
}_{\text{sample proportion}}
}
\]

And the two weights add to one!
\[
\frac{\alpha+\beta}{\alpha+\beta+n}
+
\frac{n}{\alpha+\beta+n}
=1.
\]

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Posterior as a Weighted Compromise}

The posterior mean can be written as

\[
\mathbb{E}(\pi\mid y)
=
\frac{\alpha+\beta}
     {\alpha+\beta+n}
\mathbb{E}(\pi)
+
\frac{n}
     {\alpha+\beta+n}
\frac{y}{n}.
\]

\vspace{0.35cm}

Thus, the posterior mean is a weighted average of

\begin{itemize}
  \item the prior mean $\mathbb{E}(\pi)$, and
  \item the observed sample proportion $y/n$.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.25cm}

The quantity $\alpha+\beta$ acts as an
\textbf{effective prior sample size}.

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Batch 1: Interpreting Prior Strength}

Analyst A has a relatively strong prior:

\[
\alpha+\beta = 18+2 = 20.
\]

The new dataset contains only

\[
n=10
\]

observations.

\vspace{0.3cm}

Therefore, the posterior for Analyst A remains relatively close
to the prior belief.

\vspace{0.3cm}

Analyst B has a much weaker prior:

\[
\alpha+\beta = 1+1 = 2.
\]

Therefore, the observed data have much greater influence on the
posterior.

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Sequential Bayesian Learning}

The audit continues.

\vspace{0.35cm}

Batch 2 contains 40 additional records:

\[
30 \text{ PASS},
\qquad
10 \text{ FAIL}.
\]

\vspace{0.3cm}

What should be used as the prior before analyzing Batch 2?

\pause

\vspace{0.3cm}

\[
\boxed{
\text{The Batch 1 posterior becomes the Batch 2 prior.}
}
\]

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Batch 2: Sequential Updating}

Each analyst uses the Batch 1 posterior as the new prior.

\vspace{0.25cm}

\begin{align*}
\text{Analyst A: }&
\mathrm{Beta}(24,6)
\longrightarrow
\mathrm{Beta}(54,16),
\\[0.15cm]
\text{Analyst B: }&
\mathrm{Beta}(7,5)
\longrightarrow
\mathrm{Beta}(37,15),
\\[0.15cm]
\text{Analyst C: }&
\mathrm{Beta}(9,11)
\longrightarrow
\mathrm{Beta}(39,21).
\end{align*}

\vspace{0.25cm}

Posterior means:

\[
0.771,
\qquad
0.712,
\qquad
0.650.
\]

\vspace{0.2cm}

The analysts still disagree, but their posterior beliefs
are becoming more similar.

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What Is Uncertainty Propagation?}

We do not know the true pass rate $\pi$. A Beta distribution describes
which values of $\pi$ are plausible and how strongly we believe them.

\vspace{0.4cm}

When a new batch arrives, we update the \textbf{entire distribution}:

\[
\underbrace{\mathrm{Beta}(24,6)}_{\text{after Batch 1}}
\quad\xrightarrow{\;30\text{ passes},\;10\text{ failures}\;}
\underbrace{\mathrm{Beta}(54,16)}_{\text{after Batch 2}}.
\]

\vspace{0.25cm}

\textbf{Propagation} means carrying that uncertainty forward: the
posterior from one batch becomes the prior for the next batch.

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What Changes as Uncertainty Propagates?}

For Analyst A, the estimated pass rate and its uncertainty both change.

\vspace{0.3cm}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & Distribution for $\pi$ & Mean & Standard deviation \\
\midrule
After Batch 1 & $\mathrm{Beta}(24,6)$ & 0.800 & 0.072 \\
After Batch 2 & $\mathrm{Beta}(54,16)$ & 0.771 & 0.050 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{0.3cm}

The mean moves toward the new evidence. The smaller standard deviation
means that plausible values of $\pi$ are more tightly clustered.

\vspace{0.25cm}

\textcolor{blue}{Pass along the full posterior distribution, not just its mean.}

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Batch 3: More Data Arrive}

Batch 3 contains another 150 records:

\[
114 \text{ PASS},
\qquad
36 \text{ FAIL}.
\]

Across all three batches,

\[
y = 6+30+114 = 150,
\]

and

\[
n = 10+40+150 = 200.
\]

Therefore, the overall observed pass rate is

\[
\frac{150}{200}=0.75.
\]

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Final Posterior Results}

\begin{table}
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{
>{\centering\arraybackslash}p{1.5cm}
>{\centering\arraybackslash}p{3.0cm}
>{\centering\arraybackslash}p{2.8cm}}
\toprule
Analyst & Final posterior & Posterior mean \\
\midrule
A & $\mathrm{Beta}(168,52)$ & 0.764 \\
B & $\mathrm{Beta}(151,51)$ & 0.748 \\
C & $\mathrm{Beta}(153,57)$ & 0.729 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\vspace{0.4cm}

The three posterior means are now much closer together.

\vspace{0.25cm}

As the amount of observed data increases,

\[
\text{weight of prior} \downarrow,
\qquad
\text{weight of data} \uparrow.
\]

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{}

\begin{itemize}

  \item Analyst A begins optimistic and updates downward.

  \item Analyst C begins skeptical and updates upward.

  \item Analyst B begins with a diffuse prior and responds quickly
        to the observed data.

  \item As more observations arrive, the posterior distributions
        become increasingly concentrated.

  \item The analysts' posterior beliefs move closer to the empirical
        pass rate of $0.75$.

\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Sequential Updating vs.\ All at Once}

Instead of processing the observations in three batches, suppose
we analyze all 200 records simultaneously.

\vspace{0.3cm}

For Analyst A,

\[
\mathrm{Beta}(18,2)
\longrightarrow
\mathrm{Beta}(18+150,\;2+50).
\]

Therefore,

\[
\mathrm{Beta}(18+150,\;2+50)
=
\mathrm{Beta}(168,52).
\]

\vspace{0.3cm}

This is exactly the same final posterior obtained using
sequential updating.

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{}

Which approach is correct?

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Process 10 records, then 40 records, then 150 records.
  \item Process all 200 records at once.
\end{enumerate}

\pause

\vspace{0.3cm}

Both approaches give the same final posterior.

\vspace{0.3cm}

\[
\boxed{
\text{Sequential updating}
=
\text{updating with all the data at once}
}
\]

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Data-Order Invariance}

The final posterior depends on the total number of passes and
failures, not on the order in which the batches are processed.

\vspace{0.3cm}

For Analyst A,

\[
\text{Prior} = \mathrm{Beta}(18,2).
\]

The complete dataset contains

\[
150 \text{ passes},
\qquad
50 \text{ failures}.
\]

Thus,

\[
\mathrm{Beta}(18+150,\;2+50)
=
\mathrm{Beta}(168,52).
\]

\vspace{0.2cm}

Reversing the order of the batches changes the intermediate
posteriors, but not the final posterior.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Questions}

\begin{enumerate}

  \item Why do the three analysts disagree after Batch 1 even though
        they observed identical data?

  \item Which prior exerts the greatest influence after Batch 1,
        and why?

  \item Why do the posterior distributions become more similar as
        more records are inspected?

  \item Why does sequential updating produce the same final posterior
        as analyzing all 200 observations simultaneously?

\end{enumerate}

\end{frame}

%------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Bayesian Learning}

\[
\boxed{
\text{Prior knowledge}
+
\text{new evidence}
\longrightarrow
\text{updated knowledge}
}
\]

\vspace{0.45cm}

Then,

\[
\boxed{
\text{Updated knowledge becomes the next prior}
}
\]

\vspace{0.45cm}

For the Beta--Binomial model, the mechanics are simple:

\[
\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
\longrightarrow
\mathrm{Beta}(\alpha+y,\beta+n-y).
\]

\end{frame}
