Short bio

Alexander Abuabara is an educator and academic dedicated to applying systems thinking and Bayesian statistics to improve decision-making processes ("everyone uses Bayes when appropriate; I use it even when it's not"). He earned his B.S. in Civil Engineering from the University of São Paulo (Brazil, 2001) and completed his M.S. in Production Engineering at the Federal University of São Carlos (Brazil, 2006) where his thesis focused on a mixed-integer programming application to multi-period cutting-stock planning. He received his Ph.D. in Urban and Regional Science from Texas A&M University (USA, 2022) with a dissertation exploring the use of Bayesian networks to support urban planning decisions.

Before joining the Wm Michael Barnes Department of Industrial & Systems Engineering in the Fall of 2025, Alexander taught research and analytical methods, applying spatial analysis and geographic data to better understand complex urban and environmental systems. He held instructional and research roles in the Department of Landscape Architecture and Urban Planning at Texas A&M University, serving as an Instructional Assistant Professor (2024–2025), a Postdoctoral Research Associate (2022–2024), and previously as a Graduate Research Assistant and Lecturer (2015–2022). During this time, he taught both undergraduate and graduate students and served as a Faculty Senator representing the College of Architecture (2024–2025).

His postdoctoral research, funded by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), focused on structuring data and developing models for federal assistance after large disasters. Since 2015, Alexander has been a member of the Hurricane Evacuation Studies team (TAMU/TTI/USACE), supporting research on hazard exposure, household evacuation behavior, and evacuation zone development to improve planning and risk mitigation in Texas coastal counties.

Earlier in his career (2002–2005), he contributed to logistics and forecasting model development for one of the world’s largest citrus juice companies.

His current research focuses on enhancing education in Data Engineering by teaching courses in decision-making and overseeing capstone projects that guide students through the complete data pipeline, integrating research with practical applications. Alexander is also interested in exploring how coincidences, irrational beliefs, and cognitive biases shape decision-making processes as part of a broader investigation into the psychological dimensions of risk and resilience. He is preparing a textbook to support his teaching of Bayesian statistics and probabilistic reasoning using influence diagrams.

Teaching statement

Teaching has always been one of the most energizing parts of my work, whether training colleagues, mentoring students, or leading my own classes. I teach and create educational content to help students better understand statistics, probability, and how data is collected, cleaned, and used to make decisions. Every semester is different and each group of students is unique; what worked once might not work again. I really want to be a great teacher and that means always learning, adapting, and getting better each and every day. My teaching philosophy is grounded in a sincere desire to be an outstanding educator who adapts, learns, and improves. A classroom should be a place where students feel safe, supported, and inspired to succeed. In my teaching, I work to create a structured yet dynamic environment that empowers students through guided autonomy and collaborative learning. I prioritize hands-on activities, applied learning that connects students to real-world problems and industries through practical projects aiming to build both competence and confidence. Teaching has never been a simple job; it takes expertise and patience, creativity and heart. For me, teaching extends beyond lectures; it includes mentoring, fostering peer learning, and creating opportunities for students to build compelling portfolios and professional identities. Teaching isn’t just about delivering content; it’s about being present, responsive, and always be committed to the growth of each student.

Tips on how to be productive in the work

(work in progress)
  • Stay motivated: Focus on topics that excite you (sometimes there is stuff that needs to be done but there is a lot of flexibility).
  • Train: Write one (or two) papers quickly for practice, then tackle something truly important.
  • Work hard: Time put into science has a highly superlinear effect on science output. This is because there are many baseline time expenses such as reading papers, reading email. Doing orientations, etc.
  • Fail fast: Test ideas quickly (cut corners if needed), then refine and execute well once promising.
  • Collaborate: Seek partners who can add speed, expertise, or experience.
  • Be proactive: Keep your PI updated, ask questions, and make sure your projects stay on the top of his pile.
  • Network intentionally: Know the key people in your field; use conferences strategically.
  • Be a nice person: Socialize. Lunch. Support each other.
